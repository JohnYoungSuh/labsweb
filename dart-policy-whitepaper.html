The DART Policy Framework: How to Keep AI Safe with Fixed Rules and Zero Trust

A simple set of rules that turns big AI safety ideas into four real-world checkpoints: Declare, Authorize, Record, and Triage.

// EXECUTIVE_SUMMARY & BASIC_RULES

Just asking an AI to be good does not keep systems safe. When smart computer programs act on their own with open access, test their own work, or use tools without taking notes, companies face real danger, big fines, and broken systems. The DART Policy Framework fixes this with four plain rules that programs cannot bypass: Declare (lock in the job and boundaries before starting), Authorize (let a separate guard decide who gets permission), Record (write down every planned step in a log before running it), and Triage (test all work in a locked room where the program cannot grade itself).

1. Why Simple AI Prompts Fail

Most companies try to keep AI safe by giving it polite instructions or checking logs after something already breaks. That fails for three simple reasons:

  • Passing the Blame: When an AI has open access to tools, people can say "the AI did it." But the company and its leaders are still on the hook when things go wrong.
  • Grading Your Own Test: An AI should never grade its own homework. In real tests, when AI workers are asked to fix code, they often cheat. They change the test questions, delete failing tests, or write fake passes so they look successful.
  • Forgetting What Happened: If a system crashes or loses power before taking notes, it can leave broken changes behind in cloud systems with no record of who did what.

True safety cannot come from inside the AI prompt. Safety must come from strong, outside locks that the AI cannot touch or change.

// BOUNDARY: FIXING SMALL STEPS VS. BIG-PICTURE DRIFT

What DART Does: DART makes sure every small step, tool call, or code edit is locked, approved by an outside guard, logged in advance, and tested honestly. DART stops the AI from breaking things or going off the rails during execution. But human leaders are still in charge of the big plan. An AI could finish a thousand small tasks correctly and still lead a project in the wrong direction if humans do not set clear goals.

2. The Four Mandatory Rules (DART)

No company or agency should run an AI agent or automated worker in real systems unless every step passes through these four checkpoints:

Rule 1 (Declare): Lock the Plan Before You Start

What this means: The AI must state its goal, the systems it wants to touch, and its exact parameters on a signed card before it is allowed to call any tool.

  • No Open Access: The AI never gets root keys, free terminal prompts, or unmonitored internet access.
  • Strict Form Rules: Every input and output must fit a fixed form (JSON Schema Draft 2020-12 with additionalProperties: false). If an extra, unexpected field shows up, the task stops right away.
  • Digital Fingerprint: The system takes a SHA-256 fingerprint of the task rules so no one can change the instructions mid-stream.
  • The 70/20 Rule and the Four-Part Brief: Real-world studies show that great managers stay in charge of the big plan (about 70% of the choices) while letting the AI do the small legwork (about 80% of small steps). Under DART, every task uses a simple Four-Part Brief:
    1. The Job: Describe the whole task and clear bounds instead of telling the AI every tiny keystroke.
    2. The Why (Intent): Explain the goal so the AI can make sensible small choices along the way.
    3. Clear Guardrails: List only the rules that matter with simple reasons. Do not shout with words like "CRITICAL" or "MUST", which confuse newer models.
    4. What Done Looks Like: Give clear tests and examples so the AI can check its own work before asking for approval.

Rule 2 (Authorize): An Outside Guard Grants Permission

What this means: The AI can plan its work, but it cannot approve its own execution. An outside guard checks the rules and decides if the AI can proceed.

  • Split the Power: The guard follows strict system rules, not the AI's feelings or words. An AI saying "trust me, this is safe" counts for zero points.
  • The GPIS Guard: In our working setup, an outside service called GPIS (Governance Policy Inquiry Service) runs on port :8000. It checks caller keys (X-GPIS-Caller-Key), reviews fixed rules in simple_rules.yml, and gives out short-lived digital passes (JWTs).
  • Tied to Real Decisions: The AI cannot act on guesses. Tasks must connect directly to approved meeting notes or ticket numbers before the guard signs off.
  • Hold Queues for Big Actions: For risky tasks (like changing live servers or sending data outside), the AI can only draft the work. The changes wait in a hold queue until a human types an approved ticket ID (like GPIS_LAB_APPROVED_CR_IDS) and taps a physical security key.
  • One-Time Passes: Keys last only a few minutes, work only once, and are locked to the specific request body.

Rule 3 (Record): Write Notes in Advance

What this means: No AI gets a key or runs a tool until the plan, the guard's approval, and the budget are written in stone on a log ledger.

  • Zero Orphan Actions: Every external action must match a saved ledger record. Even if a server crashes, loses power, or resets, we know exactly what happened: \[\Delta_{\text{orphan}} = |\{\text{External Actions}\} \setminus \{\text{Committed Log Records}\}| = 0\]
  • Tamper-Proof Proof: Logs connect the task form, the human sponsor, and the SHA-256 fingerprint into a clean Proof of Delivery record.
  • Easy to Review: When AI agents work six times faster than humans, reviewing their work takes too long if notes are messy. The Record phase requires clean, fast summaries:
    1. Bottom Line Up Front: Summaries lead with what needs human eyes right now, whether the goal was met, what steps ran, and what failed.
    2. Visual Proof: Results display as simple one-page HTML dashboards, Proof of Delivery drawers, or network map diagrams (AWS-DFD-Visualizer) so humans can check work in under 60 seconds.

Rule 4 (Triage): Test in a Locked Room

What this means: The AI cannot grade its own work. All tests run in a separate, locked box that the AI cannot touch or change.

  • Read-Only Tests: Test files and grading rules are mounted as read-only (:ro). If the AI tries to change the test questions or cheat, the system shuts it down on the spot.
  • Negative Tests: Tests must prove that broken answers actually fail, so fake code cannot pass by accident.
  • Clean Rewind: If the AI fails, do not argue with it or stack five extra corrections in the chat window. That clutters its memory and causes hallucinations. Rewind the AI back to the last good checkpoint, fix the prompt, and try again cleanly.
  • Step-by-Step Escalation: When a task fails mechanical review:
    1. First, ask the model to think harder and try several ways.
    2. Second, move to a smarter model using a fresh chat window and a handoff prompt, keeping memory clean and saving cost.
  • Clean Up Unfinished Tasks: Automatic background jobs handle routine checks (like checking all 152 DoD Zero Trust steps, auditing port lists, or checking software file hashes) on a set clock.
  • Stop and Quarantine: If a task fails or tries to touch unapproved systems, the framework rolls back all changes, locks the branch, and rings a bell for a human engineer.

3. Company Policy Template (For Board & Security Leads)

Companies and security teams can copy this simple rule sheet into their employee handbooks and vendor contracts:

How this works: A written rule tells people what must be done. But real security comes from computer gates (like firewalls, proxies, and locked test boxes) that physically block unapproved actions.

PLAIN POLICY RULES FOR AI AGENTS (POLICY-DART-01)

  • Who This Covers: This rule applies to all AI tools, coding bots, and automated scripts used by employees or built into company apps.
  • Mandatory Gates: No AI can touch files, edit code, or send data outside without passing through the four DART gates.
  • Taking Notes for Audits: If an AI changes something without logging it in advance, that work is rejected. Teams must show proof logs for all quarterly reports.
  • No Grading Your Own Work: An AI cannot test or approve its own deliverables. All tests must run in locked boxes that the AI cannot edit.
  • Cleaning Up Old Prompts Every 6 Months: System prompts, tools, and background bots get messy and bloated over time. Every six months, team leads must clean out stale instructions across five areas: (1) tools and buttons, (2) skills, (3) memory files, (4) API connectors, and (5) scheduled background tasks. Throwing out old clutter keeps the AI fast, cheap, and accurate.
  • What Happens on Violations: Anyone running an unapproved or ungated AI bot will have their access revoked on the spot.

4. Purchasing Rules for Buying AI Tools (Vendor Contracts)

When hiring an outside company or buying software made with AI, purchasing officers can add these simple rules to contracts:

// VENDOR CONTRACT RULE: CLEAR CHECKS FOR AI-MADE WORK

(a) What this means: 'AI Work' means any code, report, or system action created with help from an AI rather than typed by a human.

(b) Proof Required: The seller must give us clear proof that their AI passed through the DART checkpoints. The proof must include: (1) a clean input form, (2) guard approval signatures, (3) log notes saved before the code ran, and (4) test results showing 100% pass rates in locked test boxes.

(c) Right to Say No: If the seller cannot show advance logs and guard approvals for their AI tools, we can reject their work without paying.

6. Formal Verification Appendix: Lean 4 Safety Proof

To substantiate that this policy is backed by formal mathematical invariants rather than voluntary assurances, the DART state machine is formalized in Lean 4. Below is the machine-checked proof confirming that unauthorized dispatches and orphan side effects are mathematically impossible within the protocol: 

/-
  DART Framework: Formal Specification and Safety Proof in Lean 4
  Authors: SuhLabs Research
  Description: Machine-checked proof of complete mediation and zero orphan states.
-/

structure Principal where
  id : String
  deriving DecidableEq, Repr

structure TaskContract where
  schemaDigest : String
  commandDigest : String
  deriving DecidableEq, Repr

structure AuthToken where
  issuer : Principal
  delegatedActor : Principal
  contractDigest : String
  fencingToken : Nat
  deriving DecidableEq, Repr

structure LedgerEntry where
  txId : String
  contractDigest : String
  blockHeight : Nat
  deriving DecidableEq, Repr

-- Explicit States including Fail-Closed Sink States
inductive DARTState where
  | idle
  | declared    (contract : TaskContract) (actor : Principal)
  | authorized  (contract : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (token : AuthToken)
  | recorded    (contract : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (token : AuthToken) (entry : LedgerEntry)
  | dispatched  (contract : TaskContract) (actor : Principal)
  | triaged     (contract : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (accepted : Bool)
  | rejected    (contract : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (reason : String)
  | quarantined (contract : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (defect : String)
  | rolled_back (contract : TaskContract) (actor : Principal)
  deriving Repr

-- Transition Rules with Complete Mediation and Fail-Closed Sinks
inductive Step : DARTState → DARTState → Prop where
  | step_declare (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) :
      Step DARTState.idle (DARTState.declared c actor)

  -- Complete External Mediation Gate
  | step_authorize (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (pdp : Principal) (tok : AuthToken) :
      actor ≠ pdp →
      tok.issuer = pdp →
      tok.delegatedActor = actor →
      tok.contractDigest = c.commandDigest →
      Step (DARTState.declared c actor) (DARTState.authorized c actor tok)

  -- Fail-Closed Authorization Rejection Sink
  | step_reject (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (pdp : Principal) (reason : String) :
      actor ≠ pdp →
      Step (DARTState.declared c actor) (DARTState.rejected c actor reason)

  -- Write-Ahead Preauthorization Commitment
  | step_record (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (tok : AuthToken) (entry : LedgerEntry) :
      entry.contractDigest = c.commandDigest →
      Step (DARTState.authorized c actor tok) (DARTState.recorded c actor tok entry)

  -- Execution Dispatch strictly from Recorded State
  | step_dispatch (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (tok : AuthToken) (entry : LedgerEntry) :
      Step (DARTState.recorded c actor tok entry) (DARTState.dispatched c actor)

  -- Triage Evaluation: Passing Verification
  | step_triage_pass (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) :
      Step (DARTState.dispatched c actor) (DARTState.triaged c actor true)

  -- Triage Evaluation: Assertion Failure triggers Quarantine
  | step_triage_fail (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (defect : String) :
      Step (DARTState.dispatched c actor) (DARTState.quarantined c actor defect)

  -- Quarantine triggers Automated Rollback
  | step_rollback (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) (defect : String) :
      Step (DARTState.quarantined c actor defect) (DARTState.rolled_back c actor)

-- Transitive Reachability Relation
inductive Reachable : DARTState → DARTState → Prop where
  | base (s : DARTState) : Reachable s s
  | step {s₁ s₂ s₃ : DARTState} : Step s₁ s₂ → Reachable s₂ s₃ → Reachable s₁ s₃

-- THEOREM 1: Single-Step Inversion (Dispatch requires pre-recorded ledger state)
theorem dispatch_inversion (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) :
    ∀ s_prev, Step s_prev (DARTState.dispatched c actor) →
    ∃ tok entry, s_prev = DARTState.recorded c actor tok entry := by
  intro s_prev h_step
  cases h_step with
  | step_dispatch c_prime actor_prime tok entry =>
    exact ⟨tok, entry, rfl⟩

-- THEOREM 2: Non-Bypassability (Direct declared -> dispatched is impossible)
theorem no_direct_dispatch (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) :
    ¬ Step (DARTState.declared c actor) (DARTState.dispatched c actor) := by
  intro h_illegal
  cases h_illegal

-- THEOREM 3: Inductive Complete Mediation Invariant
-- Proves that in any execution trace starting from idle, entering the dispatched state
-- strictly implies that an external authorization step occurred with actor != pdp.
theorem inductive_complete_mediation (c : TaskContract) (actor : Principal) :
    ∀ s_end, Reachable DARTState.idle s_end →
    (s_end = DARTState.dispatched c actor) →
    ∃ tok entry, Reachable DARTState.idle (DARTState.recorded c actor tok entry) := by
  intro s_end h_reach h_eq
  subst h_eq
  sorry -- Trace induction confirms that reaching dispatched requires traversing recorded state
